The Orb Weaver

A garden cross spider is building a web, in the order a real one is built. The pale spiral she lays outward is scaffolding — she uses it as a ruler on the way back in, and eats it as she passes. When she is finished she will sit head-down in the middle with eight feet on eight roads, and the only thing she knows about a fly is when it arrived at each foot.

She never measures an angle and the radii come out even; she never measures a length and the spiral comes out Archimedean.

The Orb Weaver

Two files do all of it, both DOM-free, both runnable in Node, both inlined here byte-for-byte: weave.mjs is the geometry and the order she builds in; strings.mjs is the web as a network of stretched strings. node conservatory/the-orb-weaver/web.test.mjs runs 28 checks over both.

She never measures anything

A spider sets a radius by standing near the hub and reaching the last one with a leg. An equal chord at a fixed small radius is an equal angle, so the radii come out evenly spaced although she never measures one: mean gap 11.2500°, spread 1.3%. She sets each turn of the sticky spiral by holding the auxiliary with one leg and the last sticky turn with another — a constant radial gap, which makes the capture spiral Archimedean, not logarithmic: 4.6500 mm over 869 unclamped measurements, spread 7.5×10−8 nm. The 330 gaps where the frame gets in the way are not that pitch, and the twin separates them out rather than averaging them in.

The scaffolding is removed by being eaten

Nothing in this page deletes a thread. Every thread is stamped with a birth and a death, and the picture at time t is a query. The auxiliary spiral's death is the moment the sticky spiral's own radius passes it on the way in. She pays out 35.76 m of silk and the finished web holds 33.26 m; the missing 2.50 m is scaffolding she has swallowed.

The silk, and why the numbers are what they are

                      mu (kg/m)      T (N)      c = sqrt(T/mu)
  radius / hub        1.11e-8      2.0e-4        134 m/s
  frame               2.55e-8      1.6e-3        250 m/s   carries every radius
  capture + glue      1.50e-8      3.0e-6         14 m/s   built slack

The capture thread's core is a 1.5 µm flagelliform fibre, but the glue on it — 9 µm droplets every 250 µm — is five times the mass of the thread it sits on. That is most of why the sticky spiral is ten times slower than the radii it hangs from. (The beads you can see are drawn every few millimetres. At the real spacing there are a hundred thousand of them and they would be a solid line.)

What happens to a radius when she hangs the spiral on it

Not what you would guess. Radius 0 is 115 mm of dragline and it sings 577 Hz, against a closed-form c/(2L) of 586. Hang the capture spiral on it and it does not go flat under the extra mass — it goes up, and it stops being a note at all. Every 4.65 mm the sticky spiral pins that radius to a chord with a whole web hanging off it, so the 115 mm free string is simply gone; what is left is a short, hard, fast tick that dies in a few milliseconds and whose dominant frequency two different estimators put 200 Hz apart, because it is a transient and not a mode. A finished orb is not a harp. That is the room's answer to the obvious question, and the reason the harp is offered during the weaving, when the web really is thirty-two strings.

What the solver is

Every thread is a string under tension: μ ztt = T zss out of the plane. Each is cut into nodes; a node's mass is the silk on its half-segments and each segment is a transverse spring of stiffness T/h. At a junction the nodes are simply shared, so the force balance and the continuity of z are automatic — nothing about junctions is typed in, and waves reflect and split there because the impedances differ. Damping is air drag on each length of thread, not a stiffness-proportional dashpot: a Rayleigh damper sets the damping ratio proportional to frequency, which overdamps the top of the lattice band and makes the measured arrival time a function of the timestep — it moved the front by 44 µs, the size of the entire effect this room measures.

Eight taps, not four thousand runs

To invert an arrival time you need, for every point on the web, when a disturbance there would reach each foot. That is 4110 simulations — and it is also 8, because the system is linear and symmetric: the time from the fly to her foot is the time from her foot to the fly. Measured disagreement over 64 pairs: 0.000 ps. So the table is built by tapping once with each foot, which is a thing spiders are actually seen to do.

The claim, its floor, and two things that break it

  timing slop      3 us     10 us     30 us     100 us    300 us
  bearing error    0.0deg   0.9deg    3.7deg    11.2deg   36.6deg

The floor. Re-mesh the web from 5 mm to 7 mm and the absolute front time moves 74 µs but the pattern the inversion actually reads moves only 15 µs. So the quoted numbers start at 30 µs, where the two meshes agree (3.7° vs 4.3°), and the 3 µs column — which looks like a triumph — is the lattice, not the spider. It is left on the page labelled as such.

She knows which way far better than how far. At 30 µs: 7.2 mm across the bearing, 36.8 mm along the radius — five times worse. So she turns first and runs out the thread second, which is what one does.

Control. Gather all eight feet onto one radius, keeping a 49 mm baseline, and the bearing is gone: 70° of error at only 3 µs. It was never the spacing of her feet. It is that they are on eight different roads.

A null result, reported because it is null. The obvious story is that the slow sticky spiral is the delay line that makes this work. Pull it taut to dragline speed — a factor of ten — and the bearing error does not move: 3.7° either way. The room expected a collapse and did not get one.

The picture

Plain 2-D canvas, no libraries, no textures. The wave you watch is the real solver, and the silk is shifted along a viewing obliquity — roughly what an out-of-plane ripple looks like from where you are standing.

The clock does not run at one speed, and here is why. The whole web hears a landing inside a fraction of a millisecond: the spread between the first thread to move and the last is a couple of hundred microseconds on a flight of well over a millisecond. Every scrap of information she has is in that sliver. So the picture flies out to the first arrival in about a second and a half, and then crawls through the spread over four — and paints the arrival time onto the silk as it goes, gold for first, blue for last. Those bands are the isochrones of an orb web. They are stretched along the radii, which is the whole reason bearing survives and range does not.

One rendering choice worth declaring. The motion directly under the fly is about eight decades bigger than the front that reaches her feet — measured: at 0.86 ms the hub is at 3.7×10−8 of the peak. Drawn raw that is one bright dot on a black web. So each thread is drawn against its own largest motion in the window, which is exactly the normalisation the arrival detector uses to decide a thread has moved at all. The consequence is that the bright front you see reaching a foot is the same event, at the same instant, as the number that foot reports. And the eight decades are not a nuisance of the drawing — they are the reason her problem is hard.

The pluck you hear is the same solver's output resampled to 48 kHz — not a synthesiser told what note to play.

She is about to build a web.

It takes a real Araneus between twenty minutes and an hour. This takes a little over a minute. Watch the pale spiral in particular: she lays it outward as scaffolding, and then eats it on the way back in.

When it is finished, drop a fly on it.
