It is a moonless night.

There is no light in this orchard at all — not one photon of it. Everything you are about to see, you will have to shout for, and it will arrive late. Sound, please.

open your mouth

Nothing here is lit

the screen is not a picture, it is a plot of arrival time

Point a ray from your mouth into the dark and it travels 343.3 metres a second, hits something, and comes back. That round trip costs 5.8 milliseconds for every metre, and it is the only information you will ever have. So a surface in this room is black until its echo has had time to get home, and then it lights up — and everything that follows is bookkeeping on that one sentence.

There is no mesh behind the screen. One fragment shader sphere-traces a distance field, and because the eye is the bat's mouth, the distance the ray marched is the one-way path. The picture and the sound are computed from the same number. When a tree lights up, that is when its echo arrived.

The colour is the pitch

the air is a low-pass filter with the corner coming towards you

Air is not free. At 40 kHz it costs 1.32 dB every metre, at 80 kHz 2.61 — hundreds of times what it charges a human voice — so the top of your sweep dies first and dies fastest. Near things come back with the whole call in them and read cold and silver; far things come back with only the bottom of the sweep left and read amber, then ember, then nothing. That is the only thing carrying colour in this room. Drag the humidity slider and watch the depth of the world breathe: the absorption model is ISO 9613-1, checked in the twin against its own published table.

Two shouts, and one theorem

the fence posts are the instrument
FM sweep
82 → 38 kHz, 3 ms		Bandwidth 44 kHz. Matched-filtered, the pulse compresses to c/2B = 3.9 mm. The posts come apart, the arriving front is a hard ring on the grass, and the time–bandwidth product is 132 — a metre of pulse resolved to four millimetres of answer. This is pulse compression, and it is why nearly every bat sweeps.
CF tone
82 kHz flat, 60 ms		Bandwidth is 1/T = 17 Hz, so the range profile is 10.3 m thick. Press it and the orchard stops having edges — one soft bloom, no posts, no ring. But 60 ms holds 2.7 wingbeats, so a fluttering moth modulates a single echo and you hear it directly. An FM bat calling ten times a second can only sample that 45 Hz wing, and aliases it down to 5 Hz. Neither call is better. They are the two ends of one theorem.

Watch what happens to the colour, too. A single-pitch call has nothing for the air to eat unevenly, so everything comes back the same note and the whole orchard goes one flat silver — the same loss of information as the smeared range profile, wearing a different coat.

The moth hears you first

one-way against two-way, and everything follows

Your shout spreads once on its way to the moth. Its echo spreads again on the way back. So the moth is listening on a 20 dB per decade law and you are listening on a 40 dB per decade one, and that single difference decides the hunt. At a full 110 dB — and a bat really is that loud a hand's breadth from its mouth — the moth hears you at 8.4 m and you hear the moth at 4.0 m, pricing the air at the sweep's own midpoint. It gets about a second, which is precisely enough to fold its wings and fall out of the sky. Watch it do it.

Now turn yourself down. Twenty decibels costs a one-way listener a factor of ten in range and a two-way listener only a factor of three, so the gap closes from underneath, and at SL = 2Lmoth − Lbat + TS + 20 = 80 dB it crosses: below that you find the moth before the moth finds you. There are bats that live exactly there — the barbastelle whispers at around 80 dB and eats moths that can hear. The room prints both ranges live, and the whole argument is two lines of algebra out of two spreading laws.

Which makes it a game, and the game is the argument

you cannot catch anything at full voice

Fly at the moth with your mouth wide open and here is what happens, every time. At 8.4 m it hears you and turns. At about 4 m your call crosses its panic threshold, it stops flying and folds its wings, and it is in the grass before you arrive — and a bat does not go into the grass after a moth. You will watch that happen as often as you like. It is not a difficulty setting; it is the two loss laws, playing out.

So drag how loud down. Somewhere under 80 dB the panel's note flips over, and then you can close the distance on something that never knew you were there. It costs you: at 76 dB the whole world you can see is about a metre across, and you have to be nearly on top of the moth before its echo exists at all. That is the trade a whispering bat makes, and there is no way to play this room well without making it.

The moth's ear is the same equation your echo is — the same spreading, the same ISO 9613-1 air. Only its choreography is invented.

You are hearing 82 kHz

time expansion, which is exactly frequency division

An 82 kHz call is four octaves above the top of your hearing, so the room does what a time-expansion bat detector does: it plays the entire acoustic scene at 1/N speed, which divides every frequency by N and leaves every ratio and every delay alone. The same N slows the picture. So at ×16 the echo lands in your ear at the same instant the tree lights up on the screen, and the delay you hear is the range you see. Set it to ×1 and you get the honest thing: silence, and an orchard that strobes.

What is exact here, and what is not

the room's own conscience
geometric acousticsevery path is a ray. Fair while 8.6 mm is small against what it hits. It is not fair for the moth, so the moth is not a ray: it is a point target with a stated target strength of −40 dB.
one bounceno reverberation. A real orchard is full of it, and a real bat has to solve that.
the reveal frontcomputed from where you are now, not from where you were when the call left your mouth. At 5 m/s that is a 0.3 m error at 10 m.
reflectivitiesa table (bark 0.42, stone 0.62, leaf 0.09, grass 0.30), not a measurement.
the afterglowa decision, not physics. Arrival times are exact; a lit trunk stays lit for half a second because otherwise your eye would never catch it. Turn the slow-factor up and watch the front instead.
the beama circular piston of 4 mm equivalent radius — 6 dB down at 30° off axis at 60 kHz, which is about where a real bat's is. Its exact nulls are an artefact of a perfect circle, so the model is floored at −17 dB. The ears are not a second mouth: hearing is one broad cardioid, so two-way sensitivity is mouth × ears, never mouth squared. The shader runs the same Bessel polynomial the twin checks against tables.
the moth's flighta wander with a threshold on it. Its hearing is the same level equation your echo uses; its choreography is invented.

The chip on the left is the room checking itself: thirty-two rays a second, marched on the GPU by the shader and again in JavaScript by orchard.mjs, and the worst disagreement between the two printed in millimetres. The shader's distance function is generated by that same file, so this is not a habit — it is the only reason the sound and the picture can be trusted to be looking at one orchard.
