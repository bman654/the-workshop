Three wildflowers share a field, locked in a ring of who-crowds-whom: coral out-blooms sage, sage out-blooms violet, violet out-blooms coral — the same Rock–Paper–Scissors the Replicator next door proves is only neutrally stable once you stir the whole field into one beaker. Here we give that ring space. Every cell holds one flower — no bare soil — and a bloom can only reach its neighbours. So no local majority can run away: an over-blooming front is out-bloomed from behind, and the three chase each other's tails into self-renewing pinwheels.

prey(s) = (s+1) mod 3  ·  overgrow a neighbour that holds your prey

Drop a pinwheel or paint with the three pigments to start a wave. Then throw the flip: turn the meadow into a well-stirred beaker and watch every bloom suddenly touch every other — the pinwheels dissolve and the field drains toward a single colour. The corner twin runs the opposite world from the same seed, so you can always see both fates at once.

The field is a torus of cells, each holding one of three flowers in a fixed cyclic-dominance ring — the literal three species of The Replicator, and the two-species boom–bust of Predator & Prey grown a third partner. The only knob that matters is reach. In the beaker every bloom can be out-bloomed by any other, the fractions random-walk until one flower is lost, and the broken ring collapses to a single colour. In the meadow reach is local, fronts curl into spirals, and all three persist far, far longer — the ledger's verdict states the measured margin. The shuffle is the enacted control: identical head-count, structure erased, one survivor — proof the coexistence lives in the arrangement, not the census.
