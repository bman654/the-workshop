The Likelihood Sluice

A hidden coin is loaded — but which way? The same additive log-likelihood-ratio walk from The Belief Beam runs up one tall glass column here, in nats. The Beam only ever poured belief; this bench gives it two brass gates so belief can finally decide. Drop beads and the float climbs or sinks; the instant it touches the upper gate you ACCEPT H1, the instant it touches the lower one you ACCEPT H0 — and the walk latches. You don't set the gates by hand: you turn two dials, α and β, the two error rates you'll tolerate, and the gates slide to ln((1−β)/α) and ln(β/(1−α)). Wald proved the realized error rates never exceed the ones you dialed — an inequality, conservative by design. Drop a bead.

The Belief Beam · the same law, only pouring
There, the same additive log-LR slide pours belief between three vials and never stops — it has no notion of enough. Here, two gates turn that endless pour into a decision: the first moment the evidence is sufficient, the walk latches. One law; the Beam observes, the Sluice acts.
The walk is belief-beam's exactly — each bead slides the running log-likelihood-ratio by a fixed ln(Lic/Ljc), the same additive, order-free step (we assert |L − replayLogOdds(cards)| < 1e-12 for every trial, so the Sluice cannot drift from the Beam). What the Sluice adds are two barriers set by the error rates alone: A = ln((1−β)/α) and B = ln(β/(1−α)). Cross the upper one and you ACCEPT H1; cross the lower and you ACCEPT H0. Wald proved the realized error rates never exceed the dialed ones — α̂ ≤ α and β̂ ≤ β, an inequality, and the twin shows it by seeded Monte-Carlo (asserting ≤, never =). Pin the gates to ±∞ and the walk never latches — proof the decision lives in the finite gates, not the walk. The sequential test reaches the same confidence in fewer beads, on average, than any matched fixed-size test (Wald–Wolfowitz, shown empirically — a modeled ordering, not an exact count).  ·  Back to the Numbers Room
