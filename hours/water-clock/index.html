The Water-Clock

Two glass vessels, the same water, the same hole. The straight cylinder on the left empties fast-then-slow — the deeper the water, the harder it pushes — so its painted hour-marks crowd toward the bottom. Reshape the right vessel's bore to a fourth-root curve and the level falls at a perfectly constant rate: its marks tick evenly, the steady clock the ancients chased. Both fill to the same head and drain through the same orifice, so both finish at once — one in an accelerando, one like a metronome. Pour them and watch.

what this proves

The claim. Under Torricelli's law v = √(2gh), a bore whose cross-section grows as A(h) ∝ √h (a fourth-root radius profile) drops the water level at a constant rate — so equal spans of time fall equal spans of height, and the hour-marks come out even. A straight cylinder cannot: its level falls as √h, fast then slow, and its marks crowd. Every line above is read from the equation, self-tested in the pill — not asserted by hand.

What it does NOT claim. This is idealised Torricelli — inviscid, incompressible flow, no vena-contracta discharge coefficient, no surface tension, a quasi-static slow-drain limit, a massless float. We model the SHAPE LAW that makes a clepsydra keep even hours, not a real vessel's friction.

“When the sun is hidden, the estate still keeps the hour — it counts the falling of the water, and the marks it falls past were placed by the proven equation, not by hand.”
