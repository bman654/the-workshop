What you are operating

This is not a chart of a coaster — it is one. You set three things: the hoist height the bead is released from, the depth of the valley, and the radius of the vertical loop (drag its apex). When you release, the bead obeys gravity on your rail. Because the rail is frictionless, mechanical energy is conserved exactly — in the column you watch PE (height) pour into KE (speed) and back, while the white total line never moves.

The loop is the test. At the very top the rail can only push the bead inward; if the bead is going too slowly, the required push goes negative — which a real rail cannot do — so the bead leaves the rail and falls. The needle reads the rail force N(θ) = mv²/r + mg·cosθ; it stays green while N≥0 and pegs to a red zero-stop the instant the bead detaches.

The one number

Conserve energy from the release height h to the top of the loop (height 2r), and demand the top speed be just enough that the rail still pushes (v_top² ≥ gr). The algebra collapses to a single, exact line: a loop survives iff h ≥ 2.5r. That is why dragging the loop-apex moves the glowing tie-line: 2.5r is not a magic number, it is the height that geometry demands. Below it, the bead detaches at exactly cosθ_d = −⅔(h/r − 1).

Honesty

Frictionless point mass; a rigid, exactly circular loop (its curvature is κ = 1/r to machine precision, asserted in the self-test); the rail can only push, never pull (no clamp, no upstop wheels). The claim proven is precisely v_top² ≥ gr ⇔ h ≥ 2.5r and its felt consequence. It is not a real clothoid loop, not a friction model, not a wheeled car. The self-test includes a load-bearing negative control: an always-completes renderer that ignores N fails.
