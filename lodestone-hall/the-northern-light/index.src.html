The Northern Light

Drag to look around. The colour of the sky is a stopwatch.

the sky, measured

Everything you can see is one atom being interrupted

the whole room, in one sentence

An electron comes down a magnetic field line at a few thousand kilometres a second, hits the top of the atmosphere, and stops. On the way it knocks other electrons loose and leaves atoms in excited states. Then those atoms have to decide what to do with the energy, and they have exactly one way to make light: wait out their own radiative lifetime without being hit.

Atomic oxygen has two states that matter, and their lifetimes are absurdly far apart.

O(¹S) → 557.7 nm · 0.75 s O(¹D) → 630.0 nm · 117 s N₂⁺(B) → 427.8 nm · 65 ns N₂(B) 1PG · 6 µs

Both of the oxygen transitions are forbidden — they break the selection rules that make an ordinary atomic line last a few nanoseconds, and they are slow in exactly the same way a lock with the wrong key is slow. At sea level neither of them ever radiates at all; a collision always arrives first, which is why these two lines are the signature of a place where nothing much is happening. Two minutes of quiet is 300 km up — the room puts the red's half-quenching height at 295 km, and at 200 km only six per cent of excited atoms ever get their photon out. Three quarters of a second is a much easier ask: the green is still passing 83% at 95 km and does not lose half until 85.

So the red floats and the green hangs a long way beneath it, and the violet — which nothing can quench, because 65 nanoseconds beats every collision in the atmosphere — goes exactly as deep as the electrons themselves. Why the green does not follow the oxygen upward

the Barth mechanism, and the mistake this room made first

Above 200 km the atmosphere is mostly atomic oxygen — nine parts in ten. If the green line were simply "electrons hitting oxygen", the green would get stronger with altitude relative to everything else, and the sky would come out an even yellow from horizon to zenith. It did, in the first version of this room, and it looked wrong in a way that took a while to name.

The green line is not made out of oxygen alone. Most auroral O(¹S) arrives by the Barth mechanism: the beam excites molecular nitrogen to its metastable A state, and that hands its energy to an oxygen atom. The reaction needs both partners, so its rate follows the nitrogen fraction — which collapses from four fifths at 110 km to a tenth at 300. That, far more than quenching, is what gives the green a ceiling and lets the red float clear above it. Fix the target species and the ladder appears on its own, with nothing tuned: at 300 km the green volume emission is eight parts in ten thousand of its peak.

The dial is a stopping distance

why a harder beam makes a lower, greener sky

An electron of energy E penetrates a fixed mass of air, not a fixed distance — about 5.4 × 10−6 E1.67 grams per square centimetre, with E in keV. The atmosphere's column mass grows exponentially downward, so a factor of ten in energy is only a couple of scale heights in altitude: 1 keV stops near 160 km and 30 keV gets to 94. That is the entire mechanism behind the colour dial. Soft electrons never reach the altitudes where the green is made and dump everything into the thin air where the red can survive. Hard ones drive straight past the red's floor and light the green — and if they are hard enough, the crimson nitrogen border below it.

The deposition profile here is not a fitted curve. Take the range law, assume continuous slowing down, and let the electrons arrive with an isotropic distribution inside the loss cone, so one at pitch angle θ travels X/cos θ to reach column depth X. Integrating over that distribution gives

Λ(x) = (2x/n) ∫x1 (1−v)−(n−1)/n v−2 dv

and swapping the order of integration proves ∫01Λ dx = 1 exactly: every joule the beam brings is deposited. The twin measures it and gets 1 to within two parts in ten million, which is the quadrature and not the physics.

What the room does not model is backscatter — a real isotropic beam throws ten to thirty per cent of its energy back out to space — or the angular diffusion the electrons pick up on the way down. Both push deposition upward. So this room's stopping altitudes should land below the published curves, and they do, at every energy, by ten to twenty kilometres. That is the test in the twin: not "close to the reference" but "off in the direction the missing physics predicts, consistently."

The same number, in time

press Flicker

A lifetime is a lifetime whichever way you use it. The excited population obeys dN/dt = P − N/τ, so every colour in the sky is a one-pole low-pass filter on the beam, with its own effective lifetime as the time constant. Press Flicker the beam and the violet and the green strobe with it while the red barely notices — it comes up over half a minute, smooths out everything faster than that, and is still glowing seconds after the electrons stop.

This is why the red in a real aurora is always diffuse. It cannot hold a ray: anything with structure finer than a minute of motion has been averaged away before the photon leaves. The room draws it that way for exactly that reason — the fast states carry the striations and the red layer does not — and the two are the same mesh with two different lifetimes, not two different art directions.

Two observers, and both of them are right

why your photographs are redder than your memory

Switch between the camera and your eye. It is the same sky and the same physics; what changes is the detector.

A camera is daylight-balanced and integrates for as long as you like. A dark-adapted eye is running on rods, which peak at 507 nm and fall off a cliff in the red: relative to a camera, the eye is worth about a tenth as much at 630.0 nm as at 557.7. And rods do not signal colour at all — they add light without hue. So a faint arc that photographs as a crimson wall across the whole sky is, to a person standing under it, a pale grey smear.

The other half of it is simply how dim this all is. A kilorayleigh of green is a surface luminance of about 2 × 10−4 cd/m² — the same order as the moonless night sky itself. Even a once-a-decade storm barely clears a hundredth of a candela per square metre. The whole phenomenon lives below the level where human colour vision works properly, which is why the great auroras are the ones people remember as green and the ordinary ones are remembered as white. Wind the flux up and watch the colour come back.

What a curtain actually is

a sheet, and the reason it has a knife edge

The electrons are tied to field lines, so the light is a sheet — often only a few hundred metres thick and hundreds of kilometres long. Every ray you see is a bundle of field lines lit from above, which is why the structure runs vertically and never horizontally, and why the whole curtain leans: at a dip angle of 78° the top of a 400 km curtain sits 85 km to magnetic north of its foot.

Because the sheet is thin, how bright it looks depends entirely on how obliquely you cut it. Brightness goes as 1/|cos θ| through a slab, so an arc seen edge-on is a blade of light and the same arc seen face-on is a faint wash. Nothing else needs to be said about why an aurora has an edge. And the payoff for getting the geometry right instead of faking it: press zenith during a breakup and the rays converge on a single point overhead. They are parallel. That point is the magnetic zenith, and the convergence is nothing but perspective — the same reason railway tracks meet.

The world here is a real sphere of radius 6371 km, which matters more than it sounds: it is why an arc 600 km to the north shows you its top and hides its foot below the horizon, and why the far end of a long curtain sinks away.
