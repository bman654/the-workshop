Tune the sky until it makes a cloud. Two dials set the air at the ground — how warm it is, and how moist (its dew point). Lift that air and it cools; its dew point falls too, but slower. Where the two meet, the moisture condenses into a flat-bottomed cloud. Watch its base ride up as the air dries, down as it dampens, and drop to the ground as fog when the two dials meet.

One law, two ways to read it

A cloud's base is just a spread read as a height. The bigger the gap between the temperature and the dew point at the ground, the farther the rising air must climb before the two close — about 125 metres per degree of spread. Bring the dials together (spread zero) and that height drops to zero: the air is already saturated, so the cloud forms on the ground. That's fog.

When no cloud forms

Two ways the sky stays clear. Make the air bone dry (a big spread) and the base climbs past the top of the column — the parcel would have to rise kilometres, so you just get clear blue. Or put a warm lid over the surface (an inversion): the lifted parcel is cooler than the air around it, so it never rises at all — it sinks back, and nothing condenses. The 🌡 capped preset shows it: the column goes still and the cloud refuses to form.

The honest model. This is the straight-line (Espy) lapse: air cools at 9.8 °C/km, its dew point falls at 1.8 °C/km, so the base sits at 125 m per 1 °C of spread — by the model's definition (1000 ÷ 8). Real air bends those rates a little (a fuller Bolton form); we use the clean linear law and prove THAT exact, not a number we can't stand behind.
