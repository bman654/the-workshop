A ring of light, drawn by your own hand. Out there is one ordinary background star. In front of it floats a dark mass — a thing you cannot see, that bends the light passing close to it. Slide that mass across the sky and the star's light splits into two crescents; line the mass up exactly in front of the star and the two crescents reach around and fuse into one complete bright ring — the Einstein ring. Every photon you see is placed by the lens equation, not painted.

Grab the dark mass and drag it toward the star ✦. Two crescents will lengthen, their tips reaching — until they meet and the ring closes.

What am I looking at? (the geometry)

One background star, one dark mass in front of it. Light from the star that would have missed your eye gets bent inward as it grazes the mass, so you see the star not where it is but where the bent rays appear to come from — and there are two such directions, the roots of the lens equation β = θ − θ_E²/θ: an outer image just beyond the ring (θ₊ > θ_E) and an inner image just inside it on the opposite side (|θ₋| < θ_E). The dashed bullseye is the Einstein radius θ_E; one crescent always straddles outside it, one inside. As you slide the mass dead-centre behind the star (β → 0) the two images become two arcs that swing all the way around and meet, fusing into one unbroken ring at θ_E. The total brightness |μ₊| + |μ₋| climbs as you near alignment (formally → ∞ for a point source; a real ring has finite width). Turn the mass to zero and the lens does nothing — one un-bent image, no ring. That is the negative control built into the same machinery.
