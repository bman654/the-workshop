The Air You Can See

You have never seen a sunbeam. You have only ever seen the dust it is passing through — and here you can take the dust away and watch the beam disappear while the light itself stays exactly where it was.

The sun is outside. Swing it round the building and the light finds a different window — the clerestory on one side, then the other, then the great rose at the end of the nave.

Take the dust to zero. The beams go out and the coloured pools on the floor stay exactly where they were — nothing about the light changed, you just stopped having anything to see it with. The pools do come up a little brighter, which is the same fact told twice: the dust that was showing you the beam was also standing between you and the floor.

The one thing this room claims

A beam of light is invisible. Light travelling through a vacuum, or through air clean enough, sends nothing sideways to your eye — you only ever see the photons that come at you. A sunbeam becomes an object because a few of its photons hit motes of dust and bounce off at right angles, towards you. The beam you can see is a column of illuminated dust.

So: the colour on the floor is direct light — sun, through glass, onto stone, into your eye. The shaft hanging in the air above it is scattered light. Take the dust away and only the second one should die, while the first should not move at all.

Don't take my word for it. This measures the actual rendered frame, on your machine, from wherever you happen to be standing: it photographs the room, sets the dust to nothing, photographs it again, and compares the air the beams cross against the lit stone on the floor.

the dust slider goes back where you left it

Nothing here is a photograph or a model file. The nave is written as mathematics — a signed distance field: an equation that, for any point in space, answers how far is the nearest stone? The graphics card asks that question a few hundred million times a second, once for every step of every ray leaving your eye, and the piers and the vault and the ribs simply are wherever the answer says they are.

The windows are not painted on. They are real apertures: to decide whether a speck of air is lit, the room fires a ray from that speck toward the sun and asks what it hits — a rib, a pier, a mullion, or open glass. That is why the shafts are cut into slender blades by the stone between the lights, why they bend when you move the sun, and why a pier throws a shadow into the air itself and not only onto the floor.

The dust does not scatter evenly in all directions. Small particles throw most of the light forward, which is why a beam blazes when you look towards the sun and merely glows when you look away from it. Swing round until you are facing the window and watch the air catch fire.
