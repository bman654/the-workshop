The Birthday Bench

A pegboard of 365 brass pegs, one per day of the year. Seat a guest and they reach up to the peg for their birthday and hang a tag. You'd guess the bench fills past a hundred before two reach for the same peg — half of 365 is 183, surely. It doesn't. Around the twenty-third guest a peg RINGS — two birthdays collide — because the chances multiply over pairs, not guests. P(a clash by 23) = 0.5073, just over a half; at 22 it's 0.4757, just under. Pull the lever and watch a fresh party ring near 23 again and again; turn the dial and the answer rides √d.

Press Space (or the button) to seat a guest. The first peg two guests share rings.
365
The bench fills with about √d guests — not d⁄2 — before two share a day, because a clash needs only one coincidence among the C(n,2) pairs of guests, and pairs grow like n². For the ordinary year that crossing sits at 23: P(clash) = 0.5073 > ½ at 23, 0.4757 < ½ at 22 — both proved exactly against the seating process itself, no curve drawn. Two controls keep it honest: the one-day calendar rings on guest 2 every time, and the naive 183 guess overshoots the real answer by an order of magnitude (183 ⁄ 23 ≈ 8×). Turn the dial and the answer climbs as √d, the constant ≈ 1.2 — a law you feel two needles ride out, never a plotted line. ↗ The Galton Board next door — there draws pile into a bell; here they collide. Same seeded dice, a different law of the heap.
