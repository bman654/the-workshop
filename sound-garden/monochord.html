The Monochord

One tensioned string. Pluck it and a standing wave rings; slide the bridge and the pitch climbs — halve the length and you hear an exact octave; touch a node and a single pure overtone leaps out. Its overtones are an exactly even ladder you can both see (count the bellies) and hear (fn/f1 = n).

Pluck

Press the string, pull it aside, release. The farther you pull, the louder it rings and the taller it swings — eye and ear get the same excitation. A light tap gives a gentle pluck.

Slide

Drag the bridge handle along the string to shorten it. f1 rises as L falls; it snaps at the half-length so halving L gives a clean octave — exact, not approximate.

Touch a node

Tap a dotted node (1/2, 1/3, 2/3, 1/4…) to damp every mode without a node there. The lowest survivor is the forced overtone — n loops you see, n× the pitch you hear.

The string law. An ideal flexible string of length L, tension T, density μ vibrates with fn = n·c/(2L), where the wave speed c = √(T/μ). So the overtones are a perfectly even harmonic series — fn/f1 = n exactly — and halving L doubles every fn. The self-test proves this to the bit for n = 1…8, that the half-length snap doubles f1 exactly, and that a stiff string (which resists bending) goes audibly sharp — its partials are no longer even — so harmonicity is the property of the tension-only model, not of arithmetic. The string law is single-sourced in sound-garden/monochord.core.mjs; the page inlines a byte-twin of it, and pitch naming comes from pitch-core.mjs.
