The Overtone Rack

A pitch is not its lowest note — it is the spacing of its overtones. Drag twelve brass faders, each one harmonic on a single held tone, and HEAR a timbre take shape under your hand. Then pull the fundamental all the way down — the bottom rung goes silent, yet the pitch holds. Your ear rebuilds it from the rungs above.

Why the pitch survives

The held tone is a stack of partials at f₀, 2f₀, 3f₀, … — each fader sets one partial's strength. What you HEAR is the additive sum y(t) = Σ an·sin(2π·n·t), the very curve the witness draws. A sawtooth is an = 1/n on every rung; a square is 1/n on the odd rungs only.

The reveal: pull rung 1 to zero and the lowest sounding partial jumps to 2f₀ — yet the PITCH does not move. A tolerance-GCD estimator recovers the same f₀ from the spacing of the surviving rungs, exactly as your ear rebuilds the missing fundamental. The honest test: retune the rack inharmonic (partial n at f₀·n1.4) and the SAME estimator returns FAILURE — the rungs share no common divisor, so there is no fundamental to rebuild. The failure is the input, not the estimator.

self-test —

The self-test proves it EXACT: the sawtooth and square amplitude laws are 1/n to the bit, the additive sum's quarter-point is the Leibniz series → π/4, the tolerance-GCD estimator recovers f₀ with the fundamental present AND removed AND on the odd-only set at two fundamentals, and an inharmonic rack makes the SAME estimator FAIL. The physics is single-sourced to core.mjs, byte-twinned into this page, with the Node twin core.test.mjs proving char-for-char parity and that the laws are re-typed nowhere.
