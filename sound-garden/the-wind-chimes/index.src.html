The Wind Chimes

Five tuned tubes and a clapper on a cord, hung at dusk. Give the estate its air and the air becomes the wind that plays them — a soft breeze only reaches the short bright tubes; it takes a real gust to swing the clapper out to the long low one. Or take the breeze yourself, and flick a tube in dead calm.

Drag the clapper to swing it — or flick any tube to ring just that one. Arm the air below and the estate's own hour plays them for you; the breeze slider always works, muted or not.

the air dead calm

The clapper and the broad sail beneath it are one pendulum. Wind doesn't push a chime steadily — the tubes, the disc and the clapper all hang from the same hook, so a steady breeze leans the whole rack and rings nothing. What rings a chime is the buffeting: the gusting, turbulent part of the air, which here is smooth band-limited noise with one component near the pendulum's own 0.77 Hz, so the wind pumps the swing the way real air does.

The tubes hang at fixed angles, nested: the shortest and highest sits nearest the middle, and each lower tube hangs further out. That geometry is the whole rule — there is nothing to learn. A light air only carries the clapper far enough to tap the inner tubes, so you get bright tinkles; it takes a real gust to fling it out to the long tube at the edge, which is why the lowest note is the one the wind has to work hardest for. Whatever it strikes is consonant, because all five are degrees of one pentatonic scale — a scale with no half-step in it, so no two of its notes can clash. The room cannot play a wrong chord.

Each tube's voice is a struck free-free tube, not a bell: partials at the inharmonic ratios 1 : 2.756 : 5.404 : 8.933, no sub-octave hum, a ~6 ms attack and a tail of a few seconds — longer for the longer tubes. A harder strike is brighter, not just louder. And the glow you see is driven by the very same envelope as the sound, so if you are reading this with the sound off, you are still getting the whole thing: you can watch a tube ring and fade.
