The Climbing Ribbon

Blend a few apothecary pigments into one muddy ink, dab a spot on a paper ribbon, and let the solvent creep up the paper. As the wet front passes the spot, the pigments stop travelling together: each rides to a height set by how it splits its time between the moving solvent and the standing paper. The mud parts into a ladder of colours. Each band's height as a fraction of the front's is its retention factor Rf — and here is the quiet miracle: run for eight seconds or forty, on a short strip or a long one, and every band lands on the same Rf. Now slide the solvent's polarity to match the paper's, and nothing separates at all.

Its separation sibling: The Fractionating Column
Two ways to pull a mixture apart. The Column sorts by how readily a molecule leaves — its boiling point — climbing a staircase of αN. The Ribbon sorts by how tightly a molecule stays — its affinity for the paper — and reads it off as an Rf. Both prove the same tooth: make the two phases identical and nothing separates.
→

Distillation sorts by how readily a molecule leaves; chromatography sorts by how tightly it stays. Each pigment splits its time between the moving solvent and the standing paper by like-dissolves-like — a pigment whose polarity matches the solvent rides high near the front; one that matches the paper lags low. The band's height as a fraction of the front's is its Rf, and it does not care how long you developed the plate: the bench's self-test proves the ratio band ÷ front is the same to machine epsilon across stop-times, strip lengths, and two independent front laws. And the proof has a tooth — slide the solvent's polarity to the paper's own and every Rf snaps to 0.5: no ladder, no separation, because the paper alone does no work. Slide across that notch and the whole ladder inverts — the leader becomes the trailer — proof it is the stationary-and-mobile pair that separates, never either phase alone.
