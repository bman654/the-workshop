The Fractionating Column

Boil a 50/50 mix of two liquids in the reboiler. The vapour that rises is always richer in the more-volatile one — so each glass bubble-cap plate enriches it a little more, and the composition climbs a staircase up the column toward a near-pure distillate that fills the receiver. The one number that sets how steep the climb is, is the relative volatility α — set it by the two liquids' boiling points. Now drag the flame: too low and the column never boils up — it never fills; too high and it floods and washes the staircase back to 50/50. Make both boiling points equal (α = 1) and no flame, no number of plates, can separate it — the staircase collapses onto the diagonal. The receiver is the thing; the staircase is how it gets pure.

Its glass-and-flame sibling: Le Chatelier's Vise
The same hand-blown glass column and the same drawn flame. There a sealed reaction re-settles where Q = K when you squeeze the piston; here an open boil-up climbs a staircase of plates toward a pure distillate. Two ways heat and a column tell you a law.
→

The Vise sealed a reaction and squeezed it; this column runs open, and what climbs it is the difference in volatility, not the heat. Each plate enriches the vapour by the constant-α rule y = αx/(1+(α−1)x); stack N of them at total reflux and the top composition is Fenske's αN+1 separation factor — the bench's self-test proves the stepped staircase and Fenske's closed form are the SAME number to machine epsilon, over a sweep of α, N and feed. The flame only governs the transient: cold never fills, hot floods back. And the proof has a tooth — set α = 1 and the staircase is dead flat regardless of flame: the relief below reads the receiver's OWN purity (0.50), so the caption can never claim a separation the column did not make.
