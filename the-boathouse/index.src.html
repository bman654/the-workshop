What a boat is

two foils, back to back

A sailing craft is two foils working in two fluids. One of them is in the air and you can see it. The other is the hull, the board and the rudder, and it is doing exactly as much work. Everything a boat can and cannot do is the argument between them.

In any steady state the craft is not accelerating, so the force the air makes on it and the force the water makes on it are exactly opposite. That is all. Write each of those two forces as a lift across its own flow plus a drag along it, and call the angle between each force and its own perpendicular the drag angle —

εair = atan( Dair / Lair )   measured off the apparent wind
εwater = atan( Dwater / Lwater )   measured off the course

— and then, purely because two vectors point opposite ways,

βapparent  =  εair  +  εwater

where βapparent is the angle between the course the craft makes good and the wind it feels. Not an approximation, not a fit — a statement about two arrows. The room paints both of them on the water under the boat while you sail her, and the panel keeps the running total. Work the tiller and the arrows come apart, because a boat you are working is a boat that is accelerating. Let her go and they lie down along one line.

Read it twice and it starts giving things away.

the first thing

A craft driven by drag alone has no lift in the air, so εair is exactly ninety degrees — and the sum of two angles, one of which is already ninety, cannot be less than ninety. Its course is always more than a right angle from the wind it feels, and therefore (the wind triangle) more than a right angle from the wind there is. A drag machine can never make ground upwind. Ever. The barn door is in the shed. Take it out and try.

the second thing

The two terms sit on the same footing, so to point higher you must cut drag in the water exactly as hard as in the air. Put the same rig on steel runners and εwater collapses from thirteen degrees to half a one. And nothing in the identity mentions speed — which is why the iceboat makes ground to windward two and a half times faster than the wind is blowing, while a hull that has to make its own waves cannot even keep up with it.

Down the slip

the other half of a hull: what it leaves behind

This room is about the two forces that drive a boat. Everything a boat leaves behind it is the other half, and it is stranger: on deep water every wake on Earth opens at the same angle — 19.47°, whether it is a duckling or a supertanker, at any speed. Take the water away from under it and the law dies in front of you.

What is modelled, and how honestly

the parts that are published, and the parts that are mine

Published, and checked against its own source. The sail is thin-aerofoil theory: lift slope 2π·AR/(AR+2) about a zero-lift angle of −2·camber, induced drag CL²/(π·AR·e), blended past stall into the flat-plate law — which is why one expression carries the rig from close-hauled to a dead run, where it hands back a barn door of CD ≈ 2 without being told to. Hull friction is the ITTC-1957 line, Cf = 0.075/(log₁₀Re − 2)², and the twin checks it at three decades against the values a naval architect would look up. Ice friction is Coulomb at μ = 0.012; steel on ice is published at 0.005–0.02.

Modelled, and labelled. Wave-making is a saturating Fn⁶ hump. It is not a prediction of anything and nothing here rests on its shape — only on the fact that a displacement hull has one, which is why it cannot outrun its own wind. The iceboat's lateral grip limit is invented (a sharpened blade cuts a groove and then lets go). The wind in the physics is steady: the cat's paws crawling downwind across the water are the wind's texture, drawn, and the reason the wind is held steady is that a steady wind is the only kind a boat can settle in — and the settling is the whole exhibit.

The theorem is Lanchester's, and it is older than the aeroplane. Nothing in this room's core knows what a polar diagram is, what angle a boat points, or how fast one goes; all of that is output.
