The Engine Room · the fifth bench

The Pinhole Race

Put two gases in one box at one locked temperature, poke a single tiny pinhole in the wall, and race them out. The light gas always wins — not because it has more energy (at one T both share the same mean kinetic energy), but because it carries that energy in a smaller mass and so simply moves faster. The escape rates settle on an exact ratio rlight/rheavy = √(mheavy/mlight) — Graham's law, 1846. Punch the hole and watch the gold tick predict the photo finish.

The headline ratio is the exact closed form, derived from the mean speed v̄ = √(8kBT/πm) through the effusion flux ¼·n·v̄·A — never hardcoded. The live race beside it is a reduced sampling model (a flux-weighted Bernoulli thinning, the core OWNS this — not a cos-θ Knudsen integral); it is asked only to land inside its ±√N counting-error band, and we never call a finite sample a proof.

Slide the masses and the gold ticks re-split, the discs re-glow, the dial re-aims. Punch and both gauges climb — but the light one races ahead and the live ratio walks right onto the gold √-tick, inside its ribbon. Throw the red switch and the lie is plain: the gauges climb in lockstep, the dial collapses to 1, the lamp goes red — because to make the speeds equal you had to make the energies unequal.

One temperature, one energy, two speeds — that is the whole of Graham's law. The door does not weigh the gas; it counts how fast each kind arrives.

The Brownian Ratchet — a sibling Monte-Carlo measured against an honest ±√N band, the same lineage of "the sim never IS the claim, it only lands in the band". There the null is the heart; here the exact ratio is, and the sample only confirms it.
