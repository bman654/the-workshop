Click a cell to drop a stone. Connect your two walls.

Hex. Drop stones on a rhombus and connect your two walls. Gold joins top ↔ bottom; blue joins left ↔ right. The wonder: a full board can never end in a draw — fill it with a coin and exactly one colour always spans. Press Fill it randomly and watch the tie counter stay nailed at zero.

The no-draw was never the dice. Flip to the square grid and press fill: the same coin-flips, the same sweep — but now the tie counter climbs. The only thing that changed is who-touches-whom. A square grid leaves a gap a hex never can; it was the hexagon all along.

Why a hex can't tie ▸

Two opposite-colour chains can't both cross the board (they'd have to meet, and a cell is one colour) — so at most one spans. And on a hexagon the cells leave no gap for a path to slip through, so at least one must — exactly one, always. (This bench demonstrates it empirically over thousands of fills, and exhaustively at small sizes in its self-test; the topology is why — it's equivalent to Brouwer's fixed-point theorem.)
