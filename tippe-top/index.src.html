A tippe top is a truncated sphere (radius R) whose centre of mass sits a distance a BELOW the sphere's centre, toward the round side — so at rest it balances dome-down, CoM low. Flick it spinning above the critical rate ω_crit and sliding friction at the contact torques the axis over: it walks up onto its stem and spins inverted, the CoM lifted to h(π)=R+a. The Midway · the amusements district

A little top, round-bottomed like a mushroom, sits dead still on the table — heavy end down, the safe and obvious way for weight to rest. Flick it spinning and it does something that looks like a trick: it wobbles, tips onto its side, climbs up onto its skinny stem, and spins there upside‑down, its heavy belly lifted into the air. The centre of mass has climbed against gravity. Watch the glowing heart — it is pinned to the centre of mass and its height is read straight off the physics, rising as the top inverts. Where does the lift come from? Not the spin — spin does no work on the heart. It is friction, the ladder: ω > ω_crit and it climbs.

What you are watching

The top is a truncated sphere — a ball with a flat ground off one side and a thin stem stuck on. Its centre of mass sits a little below the sphere's geometric centre, toward the round belly, so the natural resting pose is belly-down with the heart low. The whole drama is the tilt θ, the angle of the top's axis from straight up: θ=0 is upright (dome‑down, heart low), θ=π is fully inverted (spinning on the stem, heart high). The heart you see is pinned to the centre of mass at height h(θ) = R − a·cosθ — that screen position IS the integrated physics, not an animation curve. As θ walks from 0 to π the heart climbs from R−a to R+a: a rise of 2a, paid for in spent spin.

Why does it climb? Sliding friction at the contact point. As the spinning top slips on the table, the friction force has a moment that torques the symmetry axis over — provided the spin is fast enough. There is a sharp threshold ω_crit, drawn as a bright tick on the spin track: below it the friction torque points the axis down and the top just settles heavy‑side‑down; above it the torque points the axis up and the top inverts. The tick sits where the geometry and inertia put it — ω_crit is set by the mass distribution, not the friction, so it holds its place as you drag the dial. What the friction decides is whether the climb happens at all: it is the rung the spin walks up. Spin alone never does this — the spin's own kinetic energy is what pays the climb, but the friction is what spends it on lifting the heart. Drop the dial to slick glass and the rung is gone; the tick still marks the threshold, but nothing can reach for it.

Two instruments make it tactile. The FRICTION DIAL μ is the rung: at the slick end the track turns glassy and the top can only wobble, never climb; at the grippy end it inverts in seconds. The SPIN KNOB ω carries the live ω_crit tick; cross it and the value badge flips from a cool below threshold to a warm climbs. And you can flick the top directly — drag across the dome to spin it up.

The neg-control you can feel — frictionless contact

Hit FRICTIONLESS CONTACT (μ=0) and the track goes glassy — but the spin knob stays fully live. Now crank ω all the way to the top and watch what does not happen: the heart holds dead flat at heavy‑side‑down, the top precesses forever, and it never climbs a millimetre, no matter how hard you spin it. That is the lesson landing in your hand: spin does zero work on the centre of mass. Without friction there is no ladder. Release the toggle and the friction — and the climb — come right back. Reversible, no ratchet.

Honesty

This is the symmetric reduced tippe top (Cohen 1977 · Or 1994 · Bou‑Rabee–Marsden–Romero 2004): the fast spin and precession are averaged and the slow tilt θ is integrated under a sliding‑friction rise torque whose sign flips exactly at ω_crit. Inversion is geometrically possible only inside the fixed window 1−α < γ < 1+α (α=a/R, γ=A/C) — an engraving of the shape, not a knob. The grid stays in the contact‑holding regime (normal force ≈ Mg) so a "no flip" is never a contact‑loss artifact. The self‑test does not fake conservation: the Jellett momentum C·n·(a−R·cosθ) is an exact invariant of the flow (machine‑ε) while n and θ sweep, and a separate frictionless‑nutation run shows the residual is genuine 4th‑order integrator error that shrinks with the step. The energy ledger closes to machine‑ε with a genuinely nonzero friction heat. Near ω_crit the flip time diverges (a top barely over threshold takes far longer to walk up) — a labelled fit, not an exact law.

All three are emergent from the integrator, never scripted — the surprise is real physics.
