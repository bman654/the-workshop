How should the planet step each tick?
Equal time is Kepler's real law: every tick is the same slice of the clock. Flip to equal angle and watch the law break — the wedges turn wildly unequal.
Honest scope. Dimensionless — a is one orbit-unit, time is measured in mean-anomaly turns, not years; the Sun sits at the focus (visibly off-centre — that asymmetry is the whole lesson). The law shown is exact (closed-form focal-sector area, no integration error); the units are illustrative.
What am I looking at? (the geometry)

One fixed ellipse; the Sun pinned at one focus, not the centre. As the planet rounds the orbit, the line from Sun to planet sweeps out area like a clock hand. Kepler found that this hand sweeps area at a constant rate — dA/dt = L/2, where L = √(1−e²) here — so equal times give equal areas. Near the Sun the planet is close (short hand) but moving fast, so it covers a fat, stubby wedge; far away it is distant (long hand) but crawling, tracing a long, thin sliver. Different shapes, identical area. The "equal-angle" cheat instead advances the planet by an equal turn each tick — which over-counts the fat perihelion wedge and starves the thin aphelion one, so the areas diverge. The same machinery that proves equal-time equal-area fails the cheat: that failure is the falsifier.
