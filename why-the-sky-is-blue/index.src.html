Drag the sun down the sky. A white beam crosses a tank of faintly hazy air. Off to the side you see the light the air steals — and because air steals short wavelengths first, the glow is blue. Look through the beam at the disk on the far wall: that is the light that survived. Push the sun toward the horizon and the air path grows; more blue is stolen, so the disk slides white→amber→deep red — live. One law: Rayleigh's λ⁻⁴.

The one law, two sights

The side-glow is blue because air steals short wavelengths first — blue scatters (700/400)⁴ ≈ 9.38× harder than red. The disk is red because the leftover light punched through the long path, and the long path stole its blue away.

Blue sideways, red through

Same air, same law — two views. The noon sky is the side-glow seen overhead (short path, you mostly see the stolen blue). The sunset is the disk seen edge-on (long path, the blue is stolen out before the light reaches you, leaving red). The blue you lose sideways IS the red you gain through.

No haze, no reddening

Turn the haze knob toward zero and the trade weakens: less air to steal blue, the disk stays whiter at every sun height. Set the medium to nothing (the self-test's neg-control, κ=0) and the disk never reddens at all — the reddening needs the air. Crank the haze up and the same sun sets far redder.

The same scattering, larger

This sky scatters sunlight, today. Step up to Last Scattering and the same word names the wall of the oldest light — the moment the whole universe stopped scattering and let its first glow fly free. Kin sideways to the rainbow and the halo, where light is sorted by wavelength too — there by angle, here by which colours survive the trip.
