The Weighted Lid

Where the Lid Comes to Rest

Next door, a meter reads the disc-drum as a number. Here there is no number to read — only a height that settles. Set a weight on the gas and the heavy lid sinks, overshoots, and the crowded discs hammer it back until it rests exactly where push balances load: y = N·kT ⁄ L. Pressure is not something you read off a dial. It is a thing your hand does.

The vessel — drag a weight onto the lid

Drag a gym-plate from the rack onto the lid — it sinks, bobs, and settles lower. Lift it off and the gas lifts the lid back up. Turn the burner and the lid climbs, lifting the weights with it — the very stroke the engines next door turn into a wheel.

The reader-of-numbers next door and this watcher-of-heights are the same gas told two ways: it reports a pressure by holding a number, and by holding a weight. The self-test proves the height it holds is exactly N·kT ⁄ L.

The honest settle

The lid is not scripted to its answer. It is a heavy wall that feels each discrete disc kick and gravity's load, and integrates. Drop a plate and it overshoots — the crowded gas hammers back — and it bobs and damps to rest. The damping is real: the moving lid does work on the discs it strikes, so its ordered motion thermalises into the gas. No drag term is added anywhere.

Freeze — the gut proof

Still the gas (T→0) and the hammer stops. Under any load the lid falls straight to the floor. That is the proof that pressure is the hammering — remove the collisions and the height that stood on them collapses.

The curve the lid draws

Turn on trace paper and every resting point (L, y) drops a dot. They fall on L·y = const — the isotherm, drawn not by a plotter but by a lid coming to rest. Switch the thermostat off and the lid rides an adiabat instead: the law P·V = N·kT still holds throughout — the thermostat only chooses which curve.
