Set a Sun and an Earth turning rigidly about their shared center, then ride the frame that turns with them — and the two primaries hang still. A massless probe now feels three things at once: the Sun's pull, the Earth's pull, and the outward centrifugal push of the spin. Add them up and you get the gradient of one field, the effective potential Ω. There are exactly five places in the whole plane where all three cancel — the balance points L1–L5, the only spots a probe can hang still. Drag a probe and feel the field tug your cursor; release it and it falls, slides, or — set down on L4 — loops a tadpole that closes on itself. The five dimples, the figure-eight contour, the drag arrow, the released motion, and the stability gauge are ALL one core: the effective potential Ω = ½(x²+y²) + (1−μ)/r1 + μ/r2 and its gradient. The five balance points are the exact zeros of ∇Ω — the collinear L1/L2/L3 by a safeguarded Newton–bisection (it never flings across a primary), the triangular L4/L5 closed-form at the equilateral vertices (r1 = r2 = 1, the 60°). Linearize about L4 and the libration eigenvalues' real part is 0 below the Gascheau bound (27μ(1−μ) = 1 ⇒ μ_G = 0.03852089650…) and turns positive above it — the calm of the Trojan points is born of a small mass ratio, not the geometry.
