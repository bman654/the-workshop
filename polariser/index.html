The Polariser

Malus’s law & the three-polariser paradox

Light is a transverse wave: its electric field oscillates across the direction of travel. A polariser passes only the field component along its axis, so through a rotated analyser the intensity follows Malus’s law, I = I0·cos²θ. Drag the dial on the bench, or use the slider.

Bench
Detector — transmitted intensity
I / I0 = 1.000 cos²θ = 1.000

P1 fixed at 0° polarises the unpolarised source — it transmits exactly ½ the incident intensity. After P1 we call that I0. The analyser at θ then passes I0·cos²θ: full at 0°, dark at 90°.

Detector — fraction of post-P1 intensity
I / I0 = 0.250 peak ¼ @ 45°

Two crossed filters (P1 0°, P3 90°) give darkness. Insert P2 at θ between them and light reappears: the transmitted fraction is cos²θ·sin²θ = ¼·sin²(2θ), peaking at θ=45° at exactly ¼ — one eighth of the original unpolarised light.

Look
Drag the analyser dial on the bench to rotate it · or use the slider. Hit Sweep to watch the screen breathe through a full rotation. The screen brightness behind the last filter is the computed intensity, exactly.
