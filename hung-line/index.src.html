As Hangs the Chain

…so, inverted, stands the arch — Hooke, 1675
self-test …
a chain, hanging
A gold chain hangs from the two springers of a stone ring, finding for itself the one curve that costs the least — the catenary, y = a·cosh(x/a). Grab it and pay out slack. Then flip it through the pin line: the very same curve, upside down, rises to stand — and the nine dry stones fade in around it. Where the inverted line stays inside the stone, the arch holds. Load it like the arch and it threads every joint dead-centre.

Hooke's law of the arch: a hanging line, inverted, is a line of pure compression. While it stays inside the stone (|e| ≤ t/2) the ring stands. The flip is an exact reflection; the loaded line matches the keystone core's proven thrust line to machine ε (self-tested).

Grab the chain and drag to pay out slack (or use the sag dial). Flip it and it rises through the pins to stand as an arch — the nine stones fade in and glow by how far the line rides from each joint's mid-line. Push the haunch load up and the working line bulges out of the stone: past t/2 a joint hinges and the span falls.
