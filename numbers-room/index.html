The Numbers Room

Arithmetic truth made visible — not a list of facts but a place you can walk. The counting numbers look like the plainest things there are, and yet they hide shapes: a tree you can descend to the best fraction, a spiral where the primes line up, a river every number falls down, a times-table that draws a cardioid, a board logic alone can fill, a pair of rods you cut down to their common measure, a heap of sand that forgets the order it fell, a sunflower whose spiral arms count out two Fibonacci numbers, a yard of pebbles you fold into a perfect square, a yard you survey into plots whose areas you must claim, a touchable puzzle whose solvability is a single conserved bit, a felt table of stones where taking stones is flipping bits, and a queen whose safe squares trace two lines climbing at the golden ratio. Thirteen benches; thirteen ways the numbers turn out to have a shape — and each is honest about exactly how much it can prove.

The Best Rational
continued fractions · the Stern–Brocot tree

What is the best fraction for π — and how do you prove a fraction is the best? Steer a vise shut around any real number: press the glowing jaw, the window slams tighter, and every convergent you pass stamps gold into a ledger — each one the closest fraction of any denominator up to its own.

The Ulam Spiral
primes & the diagonals they fall on

Write the integers in an outward square spiral, light only the primes, and they fall onto diagonals all by themselves — each one exactly a quadratic 4t²+bt+c. Two independent oracles must agree on every number, so no lit dot can lie; the running count is π(N), riding the Prime Number Theorem.

The Collatz Bench
the conjecture no one can prove

Halve it if even, triple-plus-one if odd, repeat — and every number ever tested falls to 1. Draw the tree of how they fall, or the river of their trajectories; reproduce the famous records exactly (27 → 111 steps, peak 9232). But label it for what it is: an open question since 1937, checked, never proven.

self-test ✓ · forward & backward constructions agree 0 counterexamples up to N — a check, not a proof → ♡
The Times-Table Cardioid
modular multiplication · string art ⟺ cipher wheel

Put m points round a circle and draw each i to (k·i) mod m — a times-table on the ring. The chords don't draw the curve; they're tangent to it, and a cardioid falls out at k=2, a nephroid at k=3, an epicycloid of k−1 cusps in general. And the same ring ℤ/mℤ that draws it is a cipher wheel — the multiplicative half the Volvelle never had.

The Latin Square
Latin squares · the only board logic alone can fill

Five colors, a 5×5 board, one rule — each color once per row, once per column. The clues you're dealt hide exactly one finishing, and you reach it by pure deduction: naked and hidden singles, never a guess. The room's first game — and you can pull any clue and watch the certainty break.

The Acreage
area as a claim · the only tiling logic alone can fill

A surveyed yard, parcelled into plots — each brass stone names the area of its plot. Drag a stone to stretch its claim; the tag flips teal the instant height × width = area, and it latches only when exact, over empty land. The areas admit exactly one tiling, reached by pure logic — and a claim is never destructive: the yard can only be unfinished, never lost.

self-test ✓ · unique tiling · Σ clues = area · deduction-only · bump a clue and it breaks → ⬚
The Fifteen
the 15-puzzle · solvability is a conserved parity bit

Fifteen tiles, one gap. Slide a tile and a wax seal beside the board pulses HELD — every legal slide preserves one bit, P = inversions ⊕ rows-below-the-gap, which is exactly why the board can be sorted. Then swap two tiles by hand and the seal flips to BROKEN: the board leaves the solvable half, and the solver returns no solution without searching a single node.

The Measuring Bench
the Euclidean algorithm · anthyphairesis

The greatest common divisor is not a chart of remainders — it's a thing your hands do. Lay the short rod on the long and cut off its length again and again; the last whole rod standing is the GCD, and it tiles both originals with no gap. Each cut-count is a term of the continued fraction; Bézout falls out of the very same trace.

The Sandpile
the sandpile group · the abelian theorem

Poke the sand; watch one grain start an avalanche that crosses the whole pile. A cell with four grains can't hold them and topples, spilling to its neighbours. The wonder: tumble the cells in any order — it lands on the same floor every time. Order-freedom is a theorem, and it makes these heaps a group with one eerie, fourfold-symmetric identity.

The Golden Seed-Head
phyllotaxis · φ's convergent ladder

Turn a brass dial to set the angle a sunflower drops each seed. At the golden angle the head packs into two interleaved spiral families — trace one with your finger and count its arms, and you always get two consecutive Fibonacci numbers: exactly two denominators of φ. Grow the head and they click up the ladder, never between. Turn to a tidy fraction p/q and the spirals collapse into q dead-straight spokes — you feel why the flower refuses a fraction.

The Squaring Yard
figurate numbers · second differences you fold

Why is the sum of the first n odd numbers always a perfect square? Don't take it on faith — fold it. Drag the L-shaped odd gnomons (1, 3, 5, …) and nest them; the n-th one snapping home builds an n×n square, enacting 1+3+5+…+(2n−1)=n². Then flip two consecutive triangles to tile that same square (T_n+T_{n−1}=n²), and watch a hexagonal number become a triangular one (H_n=T_{2n−1}). The room's first touchable proof — and drop a pebble and the count is an honest non-square.

self-test ✓ · n=1..200 exact · drag-to-square · drop a pebble → not a square → ◉
The Stone Heaps
Nim · taking stones is flipping bits

Three felt rows of brass stones; sweep away any number from one row, take the last stone to win — and a flint AI answers with provably perfect play. Lose twice and it shows you how: each row splits into binary place-columns (4s · 2s · 1s), and the winning move always darkens every lamp — the nim-sum. The board literally is the binary. A misère toggle (last stone loses) makes the XOR rule visibly lie in the endgame — proof the invariant is win-condition-specific, not magic.

self-test ✓ · nim-sum=0 ⟺ you lose · lamp reads the oracle · misère flips the endgame → ♛
The Queen's Long Walk
Wythoff's game · the safe squares are the golden ratio

One queen, one quadrant: slide her left, down, or diagonally down-left, and land her on the home corner to win — a flint AI answers with provably perfect play. Lose twice and the board reveals its secret: the squares you keep losing from light up, and they fall on two golden lines — the cold P-positions are exactly the Beatty pairs (⌊nφ⌋, ⌊nφ²⌋), φ=(1+√5)/2. Turn the diagonal ray off and the rails collapse onto the diagonal (plain two-heap Nim) — the golden ratio vanishes. φ, hidden in a game of slides.

self-test ✓ · cold ⟺ golden Beatty pair to size 40 · perfect always lands cold · drop the diagonal → φ vanishes
