The Numbers Room

Arithmetic truth made visible — not a list of facts but a place you can walk. The counting numbers look like the plainest things there are, and yet they hide shapes: a tree you can descend to the best fraction, a spiral where the primes line up, a river every number falls down, a times-table that draws a cardioid, a board logic alone can fill, a pair of rods you cut down to their common measure, a heap of sand that forgets the order it fell, a sunflower whose spiral arms count out two Fibonacci numbers, a yard of pebbles you fold into a perfect square, a yard you survey into plots whose areas you must claim, a touchable puzzle whose solvability is a single conserved bit, a felt table of stones where taking stones is flipping bits, a queen whose safe squares trace two lines climbing at the golden ratio, a greedy walk of semicircles that draws itself down the number line, a panel of lamps with two patterns it can never change, a balance that finds one lying coin among twelve in three weighings and no fewer, a beam of glass vials where belief pours like a conserved liquid and always sums to one, a pair of coprime gears you crank until every remainder-pair passes once, and a rhombus you fill with a coin where exactly one colour always crosses — a draw the hexagon makes impossible, and a pegboard that rings near the 23rd guest, not the 183rd, and a strip of tiles where a take-stones game turns out to be Nim in disguise — its nimbers climbing a four-step staircase nobody drew, and a ring of chairs a counting-out rhyme empties down to one seat you can read off the survivor's binary, and a tower of discs whose optimal solve is counting in binary — its odometer rolls to exactly 2ⁿ−1. Twenty-six benches; twenty-six ways the numbers turn out to have a shape — and each is honest about exactly how much it can prove.

The Collatz Bench

But label it for what it is: an open question since 1937, checked, never proven.

The Fifteen
the 15-puzzle · solvability is a conserved parity bit

Fifteen tiles, one gap. Slide a tile and a wax seal beside the board pulses HELD — every legal slide preserves one bit, P = inversions ⊕ rows-below-the-gap, which is exactly why the board can be sorted. Then swap two tiles by hand and the seal flips to BROKEN: the board leaves the solvable half, and the solver returns no solution without searching a single node.

The Quiet Room
Lights Out · solvable iff ⟂ the toggle matrix's left-nullspace

Twenty-five brass lamps; tap one and it flips itself and its four neighbours — leave the room dark to win. Each board is a vector over GF(2) and a press just adds a column of the toggle matrix, so solving is one linear system, no search. Two patterns live in the matrix's kernel: press every lamp of either and the room does not change at all — the only invisible moves. So 2²³ of the 2²⁵ boards are solvable and the rest provably impossible; deal a lone lit lamp and SOLVE jams red — no press-set exists.

The Sultan's Suitors
the secretary problem · look past ~37%, then leap

Veiled suitors approach the dais one at a time, in random order; marry or banish on the spot, no recall. Lift a veil and you learn only the relative verdict — finest yet or lesser — never a score. Slide the brass look-don't-pick band: let k pass untouchable, then take the next who bests them all. Marry and the procession halts forever; a verdict unveils whether you crowned the true best or banished them courts ago. The 1/e rule is felt, never drawn — the persisted Chronicle of Crowns climbs to a quiet peak where the band lets ~37% pass (round(n/e)); a montage holds 50 courts so the peak is reachable in one sitting.

The Coin That Lies
ternary search · 12 coins, one fake, three weighings — and no fewer

Twelve brass coins; one is counterfeit — light or heavy, you don't know which. With a two-pan balance and three weighings, find the liar and say which way it lies. Drag coins onto the pans and pull the brass release: the beam tips and two-thirds of the suspects fall away each time. A balance gives three answers, so three weighings tell apart 3³ = 27 — and there are exactly 24 cases, so ⌈log₃24⌉ = 3 is the floor and a real depth-3 schedule reaches it. Throw the 13th-coin or two-way knife-switch and the seal breaks red — three will not reach.

The Belief Beam
Bayes' rule · belief is a conserved liquid

A hidden urn pours coloured beads; which of three urns is it? Draw a bead and watch belief pour between three glass vials — it never appears or vanishes, only shifts, because the levels are a posterior and a posterior always sums to one full beam (Σ = 1). A brass rail reads the log-odds in nats, and a Gray bead — equally likely in every urn — is a no-op that moves nothing at all. Reorder the evidence and replay: it lands bit-identical, because order cannot matter. Throw the knife-switch and skip the renormalizer — Σ leaks off the 1-rule and the bead runs off its rail.

The Golden Seed-Head

The Squaring Yard
figurate numbers · second differences you fold

Why is the sum of the first n odd numbers always a perfect square? Don't take it on faith — fold it. Drag the L-shaped odd gnomons (1, 3, 5, …) and nest them; the n-th one snapping home builds an n×n square, enacting 1+3+5+…+(2n−1)=n². Then flip two consecutive triangles to tile that same square (T_n+T_{n−1}=n²), and watch a hexagonal number become a triangular one (H_n=T_{2n−1}). The room's first touchable proof — and drop a pebble and the count is an honest non-square.

The Dividing Yard
integer partitions · a number you fold across a mirror

Beside the Squaring Yard, a different fold: write n as a sum and lay its parts as a Ferrers staircase. Reshape it with your finger — the count stays n — then drop the teal mirror and every bead glides to its conjugate cell; fold twice and the beads retrace home (conjugate∘conjugate = identity). Next door, race two sieves: partitions into distinct parts vs into odd parts rise to the same height — Euler's theorem, enacted. Then let parts repeat and the distinct tower overshoots — the match is special to distinct↔odd, not a vacuous "always equal".

The Stone Heaps
Nim · taking stones is flipping bits

Three felt rows of brass stones; sweep away any number from one row, take the last stone to win — and a flint AI answers with provably perfect play. A misère toggle (last stone loses) makes the XOR rule visibly lie in the endgame — proof the invariant is win-condition-specific, not magic.

The Queen's Long Walk
Wythoff's game · the safe squares are the golden ratio

One queen, one quadrant: slide her left, down, or diagonally down-left, and land her on the home corner to win — a flint AI answers with provably perfect play. Lose twice and the board reveals its secret: the squares you keep losing from light up, and they fall on two golden lines — the cold P-positions are exactly the Beatty pairs (⌊nφ⌋, ⌊nφ²⌋), φ=(1+√5)/2. Turn the diagonal ray off and the rails collapse onto the diagonal (plain two-heap Nim) — the golden ratio vanishes. φ, hidden in a game of slides.

The Poisoned Bar

Recamán's Footsteps
a number-line walk · interlocking semicircles · OEIS A005132

Start at 0; at step n step back by n if you land somewhere fresh, else step forward — and the footsteps interlock into a restless lacework that draws itself hop by hop. The folk-claim that it never repeats is false (a(24)=a(20)=42); what's special is exact and narrower — every retreat lands on ground it has never touched. Throw the lever and watch the engine break the rule, and the structure collapse.

The Meshing Wheels
the Chinese Remainder Theorem · meshing coprime wheels

Two brass gears of coprime teeth (3 & 5), each with one chalk-marked tooth. Crank the small wheel and the marks realign only every m·n turns — in between, every reading (x mod m, x mod n) passes the window exactly once: the residue pair is a bijection. Set two clock-readings and the lone tooth that reads both at once is the unique x mod mn, reconstructed in the open ("8 = 2·3+2 ✓, 8 = 1·5+3 ✓"). Swap to non-coprime wheels (4 & 6) and a collar clamps: realignment comes early at lcm=12, and pairs with a ≢ b (mod gcd) grey out — they never appear.

self-test ✓ · bijection ⟺ coprime, two ways (closed-form == brute) · period = lcm · mn−mn/g pairs lost when not → 🎂
The Birthday Bench
the birthday paradox · √d collisions

A pegboard of 365 brass pegs; seat guests and each reaches up to hang a tag on the peg for their birthday. You'd guess the bench fills past a hundred before two share a peg — half of 365 is 183 — but around the 23rd guest one rings, because a clash needs only one coincidence among the C(n,2) pairs. P(clash @ 23) = 0.5073 > ½, just over; at 22 it's 0.4757 < ½. Pull the lever and a fresh party rings near 23 again and again; turn the dial and the answer rides √d. Two neg-controls keep it honest: the one-day calendar rings on guest 2 every time, and the naive 183 guess is struck through — off by an order of magnitude.

The Nimber Strip
the Sprague–Grundy staircase · a subtraction game that is secretly Nim

A strip of lit tiles; on your turn take 1, 2, or 3 off the live end, take the last stone to win. A hidden number rides each tile — a nimber found by the pure rule mex, never by writing n mod 4 — and the beads cycle 0, 1, 2, 3 all the way down: a four-step staircase nobody drew. Pull the split lever and the strip cleaves into heaps and the whole of Nim wakes up: you are lost exactly when the heaps' nimbers XOR to zero, and the perfect move is the one that zeroes the place-lamps. A misère neg-control (last stone loses) makes the XOR rule visibly mis-call the endgame — proof the invariant is win-condition-specific.

The Binary Ruler
Tower of Hanoi · solving optimally IS counting in binary

The optimal way to solve a Tower of Hanoi is counting 0,1,2,… in binary. Auto-solve it and a brass odometer rolls to exactly 2ⁿ−1 — the number is the trophy. The disc you move at step t is fixed by the ruler v₂(t)+1; the whole peg-config is read straight off t by a closed-form Gray decoder, whose single changed digit names the disc that moves. You-drive off the ruler and a provable floor-meter rolls your wheel past the brass — overshoot you can see, BFS-certified.
