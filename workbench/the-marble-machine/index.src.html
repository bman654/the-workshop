What you are looking at

A marble machine is an instrument whose score is its geometry. A bar's place on the wall picks the note; the distance a marble has to travel to reach it picks the moment. There is no sequencer here and no note list: move a bar three centimetres down and it plays later, and that is the only way to make it play later.

So the wall shows you the time. The dashed line is one marble's whole future, traced through the machine exactly as it stands, by the same solver that runs the marbles you can see. The ticks along it are beats of the wheel; the long amber ones are bars of four. A bar on a tick is a note on the beat. Redraw anything and the line redraws with it, because it is not a drawing — it is a simulation, run again.

Claim one · a marble rolls, and rolling is slower

A block sliding down a frictionless slope accelerates at g sin θ. A marble cannot: it has to spin up as it goes, and for a solid sphere two sevenths of the work goes into the spin.

a = (5/7) g sin θ   ·   I = (2/5) m R²

Nothing in the solver knows that number. A contact is resolved with a normal impulse and a Coulomb-clamped friction impulse that drives the contact point to rest; rolling is what emerges. Measured against the closed form over 8–45°, the worst error is 0.09 %. And a slider racing a roller down the same ramp wins by √(7/5) = 1.1832 — measured 1.1830.

Claim two · a bar's pitch is its length

These are uniform steel bars, free at both ends, struck in the middle and resting on cords at the two points that do not move — 22.42 % in from each end, which is where you will see the cords drawn.

f₁ = (β₁L)² · t √(E/ρ) / (2π √12 · L²),   β₁L = 4.7300

which is 1/L². So on this wall a bar's length is not a free parameter: draw one and its note is already decided, and an octave down is 1.414 times longer, not twice — the opposite of a string. Load the ladder, look at the rack, and measure it off the screen.

The voices are rendered from the same formula, with the real free-free partials 1 : 2.756 : 5.404 : 8.933. Those ratios are not harmonics, and that is exactly why a glockenspiel sounds like a glockenspiel and not a piano. (A concert marimba undercuts its bars to pull the second partial onto a harmonic; these are plain bars, so they keep the ideal ratios.)

The same runSelfTest() runs in Node (mill.test.mjs, 19/19) and in this page, so the pill and the twin cannot drift apart. The Node twin also renders the audio to a WAV and reads it back with tools/audio-lens: one C5 bar comes out at 522.3 Hz — C5, three cents flat, with a second peak at 1442.9 Hz, which is 2.764× the fundamental against the 2.7565 the free-free bar demands. And the whole of the ladder, rendered and read back cold, gives eight onsets for eight bars — 0.779 1.056 1.312 2.069 2.475 2.827 3.147 3.317 s, against the tracer's 0.813 1.090 1.341 2.093 2.500 2.853 3.174 3.342. Every one inside 34 ms, and the same lead each time, which is the detector's own 10.7 ms hop. The sound the page makes lands where the geometry says it will.

The honest limit

Alone, a marble's rhythm is exact — the same wall plays the same times, bit for bit. A crowd is not exact. Marbles collide, and with five more released behind it at spacings from 0.16 to 0.42 s, the first marble's notes move by up to —. That number is measured by the self-test and printed here — it is not typed into the prose. That is a real machine's real problem and the room does not hide it: run the wheel fast and you will hear the beat soften.

Where the coordinates came from

Every shipped machine was placed by the tracer, not by me: a maker's tool drops each next part exactly where a traced marble crosses the chosen height, so a shipped machine cannot be one that misses its own bars. The self-test re-runs all three and insists each reaches the trough and plays.
