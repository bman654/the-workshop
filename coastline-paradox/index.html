↗ from the Cartographer

Pick up the dividers — the compass with two pins. Set its opening, then walk it tip-over-tip along the shore. Each step is one ε; the length you trace is the coast's length. This is Richardson's 1961 question made physical: how long is it?

Open the dividers wider and they stride across the wiggles — short coast. Pinch them smaller and they thread every inlet — the length keeps growing. That growth rate is a fractal dimension D, and you earn it: it's the slope of the line you just walked.

The realm
Roughness ⟶ dimension

Higher gain keeps more fine detail ⇒ a crinklier coast. In theory the level set of an fBm has dimension D = 2 − H with H = −log₂(gain).

Two rulers, one D

The divider you walk drops one point on the rail per stride-set: its length L(ε) ∝ ε1−D diverges, and the slope you trace gives D = 1 − slope. Box-counting runs automatically as a second, unrelated ruler — N(ε) ∝ (1/ε)D. No shared code; they land on the same D.

The slider's predicted 2−H is the idealized fBm value; the real realm is warped, masked & islanded, so the measured D runs tamer — but tracks the slider, and lands right where real coasts do (D≈1.15–1.25).
