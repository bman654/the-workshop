The Coin That Lies

Twelve brass coins; one is counterfeit — and you don't even know if it weighs light or heavy. With a two-pan balance and three weighings, find the liar and say which way it lies. The trick is that a balance gives three answers — left down, level, right down — so each weighing is one question with three answers, and three of them tell apart 3³ = 27 possibilities. There are exactly 24 here (12 coins × light-or-heavy), and 9 < 24 ≤ 27 — so three is the floor, and a real schedule reaches it. Load the pans, pull the release, watch two-thirds of the suspects fall away each time.

The Source Dial
There, symbols packed into bits (log₂); here, a question with three answers (log₃). A weighing is one question with three answers — information in trits; a code packs symbols into bits. Same idea, different base.
A two-pan balance is a ternary instrument — three outcomes per weighing — so W weighings tell apart at most 3W cases. Twelve coins, each possibly light or heavy, make 24 cases; since 3² = 9 < 24 ≤ 27 = 3³, the floor is ⌈log₃24⌉ = 3 — and a concrete depth-3 schedule, built here and verified leaf-by-leaf, actually reaches it. Add a thirteenth coin and the best first weighing leaves a branch of ten cases > 9 — no schedule of three can resolve it. Collapse the balance to a two-way tips / level reading and it is a bit, not a trit: ⌈log₂24⌉ = 5 > 3.  ·  Back to the Numbers Room
