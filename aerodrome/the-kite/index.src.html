how high it is flying
angle you see— angle at the kite— the forces say— ceiling, atan(L/D)—

Above the ceiling — and it cannot stay. Hauling in throws a kite up past atan(L/D) for a few seconds by making its own wind; let it settle and it falls back under the line. The ceiling is a statement about steady flight.

The black needle is where the kite is. The hatched band is sky it cannot reach: atan(L/D), the same number as a glider's glide ratio. The green line is what the aerodynamics predict for the line's angle at the kite — the room measures that angle off the string as well, and the two are printed above.

Trim walks the tow knot along the spine — that, and nothing else, is what sets the angle of attack. Too far forward and the sail goes flat and sinks; too far back and it flies at a stall and hauls like a mule. The best height is in the middle, and you can hear it in the line.

what is actually running
  1. One Verlet world. The kite is four point masses in a rigid quadrilateral, the line is twenty more, the tail is ten. Nothing here is a rigid-body integrator bolted on the side: the pitching, the bridle and the tail's moment all fall out of the same distance constraints that hold the string together.
  2. The sail is a flat plate. The resultant is normal to it with CN = 2 sin α, applied at a centre of pressure that walks aft as the plate comes side-on, and split between nose and tail by the lever rule. That split is the pitching moment.
  3. The line is heavy and draggy. Real mass per metre, real cylinder cross-flow drag. That is the whole reason it bows.
  4. The wind is the log law over mown grass, U(z) = U10 ln(z/z₀)/ln(10/z₀), z₀ = 3 cm — so it is windier at the kite than at your hands, and the room says by how much.
the closure — two instruments that never speak

Steady flight is one line of Newton: whatever the air and gravity do to the kite, the line must undo. So the direction of the line where it meets the kite is fixed by tan φ = (L − mg)/D. The room reads φ off the solver's node positions and the right-hand side off the aerodynamic model, and prints both. They agree to about a quarter of a degree, and they have no way of talking to each other.

honest about the model

Two dimensions. The kite cannot yaw, roll, or dart about the sky. That darting is the other thing a tail cures, and this room does not claim it — here a tail shows up as drag and as damping, both of which you can measure. The sail is rigid and flat: no billow, no spar bend, no luffing. The line is inextensible by projection rather than by stiffness; the twin holds the residual stretch under a tenth of a percent. One number, CD0 = 0.06, stands in for skin friction, the spars and the bridle. The centre-of-pressure walk is an interpolation between two limits that are exact (quarter chord head-on, mid-chord side-on) and nothing between them is measured. The grass, the clouds and the haze are drawn, not solved.

the sound is a measurement

A cylinder in cross-flow sheds vortices at the Strouhal frequency, f = 0.2 U/d. A one-millimetre line in eight metres a second sings at about 1.7 kHz — the thrum every kite flyer has heard. The room plays that note and no other, and the self-test renders the audio graph offline and measures the peak to prove it.

The ceiling — angle flown against atan(L/D)

Walk the tow knot from nose to mid-spine. The room settles a kite at each stop, and draws where it flies against the ceiling its own lift-to-drag allows.

The long line — what the bow costs you

Ten metres to eighty. The kite climbs the whole way and the angle falls the whole way, and the difference is the line's own weight and drag.
