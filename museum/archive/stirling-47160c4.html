The Stirling Cycle

The same ceiling, a different machine. Carnot joins its two isotherms with curved adiabats; the Stirling engine joins them with two isochores — straight constant-volume legs — and a regenerator that catches the heat dumped on the way down and hands it back on the way up. Turn the regenerator's effectiveness ε up to 1 and a wholly different loop reaches the very same wall: η = 1 − Tc/Th.

The cost of an imperfect ε

ΔSuniverse0.000 J/K

A Stirling engine is not a toy: it is a real, buildable closed-cycle machine — it runs your fridge's cryocooler and a submarine's silent power plant. The trick that lets it reach Carnot's wall is the regenerator: on the cooling isochore the gas sheds |Qv| of heat into a thermal sponge, and on the warming isochore it draws that very heat back. When ε = 1 the reservoirs never see those isochoric heats at all — the engine takes in only the isothermal heat, and η climbs to the ceiling Carnot draws. Let ε fall and the regenerator drops some heat across the finite gap: the red ΔSuniverse opens, the gold bar sinks below the dashed ceiling, and the gap you see is the heat the reservoir now has to make up.
